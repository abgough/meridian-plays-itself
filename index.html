      How It Plays

      The beam
      A line from the center sweeps clockwise like a radar. Each plane it passes plays one note.
      Reach and tempo
      The beam reaches out until it has about two dozen airborne planes to play, from 60 miles on a busy afternoon to nearly 300 in the middle of the night. It sweeps faster when there are few planes and slower when there are many, so the music stays between about 40 and 100 notes a minute.
      Harmony
      Each quarter-turn of the beam moves to the next chord of a short progression, and every plane plays a tone of the current chord, so the notes always fit together. A soft pad holds the chord underneath.
      Size
      Sets the voice. Big jets are the bass, airliners carry the melody on bells, small planes and business jets pluck high, and helicopters tap softly.
      Distance
      Close planes sing higher, louder, and drier. Faraway planes sound lower, softer, and farther off in the echo.
      Altitude
      Planes at cruise ring long and airy; planes low near an airport play short, close notes.
      Coming or going
      A plane heading toward you scoops up into its note, bright. One heading away sags a little at the end and sounds darker, like a train passing.
      Where it is
      Planes east of you sound in your right ear and planes to the west in your left.
      Make it yours
      Open Tune the sweep to set how far the beam reaches, how fast it plays, what sets the pitch, and the scale, key, and sound. On the beat locks the notes to a tempo. Tap any plane to see its callsign, type, altitude, and speed. Zoom out to see the wider sky, or drag the map around; Reset view brings the beam back to center.

      Aircraft positions come from adsb.lol, a community network of volunteer ADS-B receivers. The page asks for everything within about 290 miles of your rough area every 15 seconds, and everyone listening to the same area shares that answer. The page plays it back 45 seconds behind real time, which lets it place each plane between two known positions.

      Receivers sit on land, so over open ocean or very remote areas the beam can come up short. Place search by Photon and OpenStreetMap. Times in the list are the real moments the beam reached each plane, in your local time. Nothing is pre-recorded.
